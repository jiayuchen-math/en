Studying Model Theory

Start here · Choose a text · Practice · Check your understanding · Continue · Full reading list

Model theory studies structures through what first-order formulas can detect. The distinction between a substructure and an elementary substructure is an early place where algebraic intuition needs checking. Pillay's short notes begin with embeddings, compactness and types; Marker's book provides a longer graduate route connecting these constructions with algebraic applications. The short route suits a focused review, while the longer route needs time for examples and the additional theory.

What to work on

  • Compare substructures, elementary substructures and elementary equivalence.
  • Apply the Tarski-Vaught test in a concrete example.
  • Use compactness to realize a finitely satisfiable set of formulas in an extension.
  • Explain how quantifier elimination describes definable sets.

A starting text

Lecture Notes - Model Theory (Math 411) — Anand Pillay. Section 1, Notation and review, printed pages 1-14: emphasize elementary embeddings, the Tarski-Vaught test and compactness. Then read the opening of section 2, Types, omega-saturation and quantifier elimination, beginning on page 15, through the back-and-forth criterion.

Choosing what to read

Evaluate the same formula in a structure and a candidate substructure before applying an elementary-substructure test. For a type, separate finite satisfiability, consistency and realization in the particular model. Work through dense linear orders with a finite partial map, and explain how each extension step uses density and the lack of endpoints. Bring the elementary diagram into a compactness construction explicitly when the desired extension must preserve more than the original theory.

Try these before moving on

Try a proof or calculation before opening the hint. These solutions address the checks below, not the exercises in the linked textbooks.

  1. In the language with one binary relation <, use back-and-forth to show that any two countable dense linear orders without endpoints are isomorphic.

    A hint

    Alternate between including the next unused element of the first order in the domain and the next unused element of the second in the range.

    Work through the solution

    Let AAA and BBB be nonempty countable dense linear orders without endpoints. They are infinite; enumerate them as a0,a1,…a_0,a_1,\ldotsa0​,a1​,… and b0,b1,…b_0,b_1,\ldotsb0​,b1​,…. Start with the empty increasing finite partial bijection. At an even stage, take the first aia_iai​ outside its domain. The finitely many existing domain points place aia_iai​ in a cut: between two consecutive points, below all of them, or above all of them. Choose an unused point of BBB in the corresponding cut and extend the map. Density and absence of endpoints make every such interval or ray infinite, so avoiding the finitely many used points is possible.

    At an odd stage apply the same argument to the inverse map, including the first unused bjb_jbj​ in the range. Each extension preserves and reflects order. The union of these compatible finite maps is a function defined on all of AAA and onto all of BBB, by the alternating enumeration requirement. It is injective and preserves and reflects <<<, since every pair occurs at some finite stage. It is therefore an isomorphism.

    If this was the sticking point:

    • Mathematical Logic & Computability — Distinguish an isomorphism of structures from elementary equivalence.
    • Proofs & Mathematical Writing — Review recursive constructions and why a union of compatible partial functions is a function.

Already comfortable with this? Skip section 1 if you can use elementary diagrams and the Tarski-Vaught test correctly. Skip this entry if you can also prove the back-and-forth result and distinguish realizing a type in the original model from realizing it in an elementary extension.

With r=2r=2r=2, many starting values move toward the same fixed point; other parameter choices produce very different behavior. Even before proving anything, there are several questions to separate. Does one orbit converge? Do nearby orbits stay close? Which sets remain invariant? What proportion of time does a typical orbit spend in a specified region? Dynamical systems connects these questions, but the methods needed for stability and for long-term statistics are different.

Start with a map

For a first project, use Gerald Teschl's Ordinary Differential Equations and Dynamical Systems, the author-posted preliminary version. Chapter 10 begins with the logistic equation and develops fixed and periodic points. Solve the fixed-point equation yourself, then compute the derivative at each fixed point and investigate what it predicts. Keep the parameter and initial value explicit. A finite list of iterates can suggest an attracting cycle; showing that a neighborhood is carried closer to that cycle requires an argument. The distinction becomes especially important near a parameter where stability changes.

A reader entering through discrete maps can begin there without reading every earlier chapter on differential equations. Calculus, proof-based real analysis, and linear algebra supply the first tools. Check whether you can use the mean value theorem to prove contraction near a fixed point when the derivative has absolute value less than one. If you already know local stability and periodic orbits, sample those exercises and move to Chapter 11 on one-dimensional dynamics and symbolic descriptions. Review metric spaces and compactness when limit sets and continuity arguments become central.

Flows give another entrance

Flows provide another entrance. If your motivating example is a pendulum or a population governed by a differential equation, read Teschl's Sections 1.3–1.5, the existence and uniqueness material in Chapter 2, and Chapter 6 on flows, invariant sets, and stability. An explicit solution is sometimes available, but a phase portrait can answer qualitative questions even when a formula is not. This branch also explains why the existence interval and uniqueness hypotheses belong in the discussion: before asking where an orbit goes, establish that the evolution rule defines it. You can postpone specialized boundary-value material when it does not support your chosen project.

From individual orbits to averages

The measurable branch changes the emphasis from individual trajectories to distributions of trajectories. Charles Walkden's Ergodic Theory notes offer a route through uniform distribution, invariant measures, ergodicity, recurrence, and Birkhoff's theorem. Begin with Sections 1–4 and work with a circle rotation. A rational rotation gives finite periodic orbits; an irrational rotation raises questions about visits to intervals. Before Sections 5 and 9–10, become comfortable with sigma-algebras, integration, and statements holding almost everywhere. Functional analysis can support operator methods later, but it need not delay the first examples.

Use the formula μ(T−1A)=μ(A)\mu(T^{-1}A)=\mu(A)μ(T−1A)=μ(A) to check that a proposed measure is invariant. Then ask the separate question of ergodicity. An invariant measure alone does not force time averages to agree with one global space average. Walkden's Section 10 states the general Birkhoff theorem using conditional expectation onto invariant information, followed by the ergodic specialization. Read the conclusion with its integrability and almost-everywhere qualifications intact. Similarly, recurrence does not mean that every trajectory returns to its exact starting point, nor does a visually irregular orbit by itself establish a chosen definition of chaos.

A good stopping point for this route is a comparison of two systems using explicit orbits, invariant sets, and an invariant measure where available. From there, follow stability and bifurcation toward smooth dynamics, coding toward symbolic dynamics, orbit averages toward ergodic theory, or Hamiltonian examples toward symplectic geometry. Carry one worked system into the new subject. It will show which extra structure the new language remembers and which new questions it makes possible.

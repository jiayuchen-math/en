A mathematics book is not a novel, and reading every sentence in order is not the only way to learn from it. A more useful question is: what should you be able to do after this section? Sometimes the answer is a calculation. Sometimes it is a proof, a construction, or an example showing why a hypothesis matters. Give the reading a small mathematical destination.

## Choose a question before choosing three books

Suppose you want to understand uniform convergence. A workable destination is to explain why a pointwise limit of continuous functions need not be continuous, and why uniform convergence changes the conclusion. That is narrower than “learn real analysis,” but it gives you a way to notice progress.

Choose one main account of that question. Look at its examples and exercises, not just its table of contents or reputation. If a proof uses a tool you do not know, find the missing tool; you need not restart an entire course. A second book earns its place when it explains a particular difficulty differently. Switching books whenever an argument becomes demanding can leave the same gap untouched.

## Give each definition somewhere to live

After a definition, write down one example you can check yourself. Then change one assumption and see what breaks. For a linear map, try differentiation on polynomials. For compactness, compare a closed interval with an open one. For an algebraic variety, calculate with one equation before taking on a general construction.

Pay attention to the order of the quantifiers. In uniform continuity, the same $\delta$ must work at every point. In ordinary continuity, the allowable neighborhood may depend on the point. Writing the two statements side by side is more useful than memorizing the word “uniform.” The [proof-writing checks](/en/notes/reading-notes/library/foundations/proofs.html#check-quantifier-negation) give a small place to practise this habit.

## Read a proof twice, for different reasons

On the first pass, locate the assumptions, the desired conclusion, and the main construction or estimate. Ask where the important hypothesis is used. On the second pass, close the book and reconstruct the argument. When reconstruction fails, return to that particular step rather than copying the whole page.

For example, subtracting the proposed limit from a sequence may turn the entire problem into the estimate $5/(n+2)<\varepsilon$. The important move is the subtraction; the remaining choice of $N$ makes the estimate precise. The [worked analysis checks](/en/notes/reading-notes/library/analysis/real-analysis.html#check-an-explicit-epsilon-n-proof) show both parts. A good note records the move and the reason it works, not only the final algebra.

## Use a hint without losing the problem

Before looking at a hint, record what you tried and where it stopped. After reading the hint, put it away and continue. If you need the full solution, read until the first idea that was missing, then try to finish without it. Later, solve the problem again from a blank page. Being able to follow a solution is useful, but it is a different task from producing one.

When you are stuck, make the object smaller or more explicit: a two-dimensional vector space, a finite group, a particular sequence, an affine curve. A failed attempt can still identify the exact statement you need. Keep that statement as a question; it makes asking for help much easier than “I do not understand the chapter.”

## Move on with a short record of what remains

Before the next section, check whether you can state the central definitions, do one representative calculation or proof, and name an example where a hypothesis cannot be omitted. These are prompts for deciding what to review, not a requirement to solve every exercise.

Keep three short lists: arguments you can reconstruct, gaps you intend to return to, and questions you would like the next chapter to answer. If a gap blocks the next argument, repair it now. If it concerns a side branch, leave a precise note and continue. The [learning paths](/en/resources/library-paths/) and subject preparation reviews can help choose that next step without turning the whole library into a compulsory syllabus.

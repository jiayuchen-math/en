## Overview

A Lie group is simultaneously a group of transformations and a smooth space of parameters. Rotations illustrate both roles: they can be composed, and small rotations can be varied continuously and differentiated. The compatibility between these structures lets a local linear object, the Lie algebra, describe infinitesimal group behavior without encoding every global feature of the group.

## Prerequisites

Use the group axioms, smooth maps between manifolds, tangent spaces, and vector fields. Matrix differentiation provides useful examples. The tangent space at the identity is a vector space; group multiplication is a different operation on the manifold itself.

## Definition and conventions

A real Lie group is a group $G$ equipped with a finite-dimensional Hausdorff, second countable smooth manifold structure such that
$$
m:G\times G\to G,\quad m(g,h)=gh,
\qquad i:G\to G,\quad i(g)=g^{-1}
$$
are smooth. Manifolds here have no boundary. Neither compactness nor connectedness is assumed. Countable discrete groups qualify as zero-dimensional Lie groups; an uncountable discrete group fails the second-countability convention used here.

For each $g$, left translation $L_g(h)=gh$ is a diffeomorphism with inverse $L_{g^{-1}}$. The tangent space $\mathfrak g=T_eG$ becomes a Lie algebra by identifying $v\in\mathfrak g$ with the left-invariant vector field $X_v(g)=d(L_g)_e v$ and setting $[v,w]=[X_v,X_w]_e$. Choosing right-invariant fields instead changes the corresponding bracket sign.

## Intuition

Translation carries information at the identity to every other point. Infinitesimal motions are vectors in $\mathfrak g$, and their bracket measures the leading failure of motions to commute. This does not require the group itself to be a vector space, or its product to be addition.

The distinction between local and global structure is visible in $\mathbb R$ and $S^1$. Their Lie algebras are both one-dimensional with zero bracket, but one group is noncompact and the other compact. The infinitesimal data do not record the circle's periodic identification.

## Examples and non-examples

The invertible real matrices $\operatorname{GL}(n,\mathbb R)$ form an open subset of $\mathbb R^{n^2}$. Matrix multiplication is polynomial in the entries. Inversion is smooth because each inverse entry is a cofactor divided by the nonzero determinant. These observations verify the Lie group definition directly. Its Lie algebra is the space of all matrices with bracket $[A,B]=AB-BA$.

The circle $S^1=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z|=1\}$ is a Lie group under multiplication. The map $t\mapsto e^{it}$ is a homomorphism from the additive real line, with kernel $2\pi\mathbb Z$. It is locally invertible near zero but not globally injective.

For $\operatorname{SO}(n)$, differentiating $A(t)^TA(t)=I$ at $A(0)=I$ gives $A'(0)^T+A'(0)=0$. Thus its Lie algebra consists of skew-symmetric matrices. All square matrices together are not a group under multiplication: singular matrices lack inverses.

The additive rationals with their usual subspace topology are a topological group but not a Lie group. Every interval neighborhood contains nonrational gaps and is disconnected, so no positive-dimensional Euclidean chart is possible; the topology is also nondiscrete, excluding dimension zero.

## Important properties

The exponential map sends $v\in\mathfrak g$ to the time-one point of the one-parameter subgroup generated by $X_v$. Left-invariance extends its locally defined flow for all time using translations. Since $d\exp_0$ is the identity, the inverse function theorem makes $\exp$ a diffeomorphism between neighborhoods of zero and $e$. This is a local assertion, as the circle example demonstrates.

Every Lie group has a trivial tangent bundle. **Proof sketch:** choose a basis of $T_eG$ and translate it by $dL_g$. These smooth vector fields form a basis at every point and hence give a global frame.

The closed subgroup theorem says a subgroup closed in a finite-dimensional Lie group is an embedded Lie subgroup. Closedness is a substantive hypothesis for this embedded conclusion; an abstract subgroup cannot automatically be treated as a submanifold with its subspace topology.

## Connections and further reading

Lie groups act smoothly on manifolds and organize their symmetries. Their exponential map is defined from group structure; its agreement with a Riemannian exponential requires additional metric conditions. [Meinrenken, §10](https://www.math.toronto.edu/mein/teaching/LectureNotes/rieall.pdf) develops the inspected group, bracket, and exponential constructions.

## Overview

A probability space is the mathematical specification of a random experiment. It separates three questions: what can happen, which questions about the outcome are measurable, and how likely their answers are. This separation works for finite experiments and for infinite objects such as a whole sequence of coin tosses.

The word “space” refers to the model, not necessarily a geometric region. Different spaces can describe the same observed distribution, while different choices of events on one set can describe different information.

## Prerequisites

One needs sets, complements, countable unions, and nonnegative series. The sigma-algebra requirement is useful even before learning integration: it tells us which combinations of observable questions remain legitimate questions. Measure theory explains the general construction, but finite examples require only sums.

## Definition and conventions

A probability space is a triple $(\Omega,\mathcal F,P)$. The nonempty set $\Omega$ contains outcomes. The sigma-algebra $\mathcal F$ contains $\Omega$ and is closed under complements and countable unions. Its members are events. The map $P:\mathcal F\to[0,1]$ satisfies $P(\Omega)=1$ and, for pairwise disjoint events,
$$
P\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P(A_n).
$$

Completeness is an additional convention: every subset of a probability-zero event must then be measurable. It is not part of the definition used here. On a finite space one usually chooses all subsets, but a smaller sigma-algebra is allowed.

## Intuition

Think of $\mathcal F$ as the collection of questions the model can answer. Knowing only whether an outcome lies in one fixed set generates a coarse collection of questions. Assigning probabilities then supplies a consistent weighting of those answers. Countable additivity ensures that splitting an event into countably many mutually exclusive alternatives preserves its total weight.

## Examples and non-examples

Take $\Omega=\{a,b,c,d\}$, all subsets as events, and point weights $1/2,1/4,1/8,1/8$. Then
$$
P(\{b,c,d\})=\frac14+\frac18+\frac18=\frac12.
$$
Equal weights are unnecessary. If only membership in $\{a,b\}$ is observed, the four-set sigma-algebra generated by that set gives a different, coarser model.

There is no uniform probability on the positive integers assigning the same mass $p$ to every singleton. If $p>0$, finite unions eventually exceed mass one; if $p=0$, countable additivity makes the whole space have mass zero. A list of possible outcomes without an event collection and probability assignment is therefore not yet a probability space.

## Important properties

Disjoint decomposition gives $P(A^c)=1-P(A)$ and $P(\varnothing)=0$. If $A\subseteq B$, writing $B$ as $A$ plus its disjoint remainder proves monotonicity. For increasing events $A_n$, split their union into successive differences to prove $P(\bigcup_n A_n)=\lim_nP(A_n)$. This proof idea explains why countable additivity controls limits, not just finite counting.

## Connections and further reading

Random variables are measurable functions on this space; their distributions transport its probabilities to a numerical space. Independence and conditioning add relations between its events. [Lawler, Section 1](https://www.math.uchicago.edu/~lawler/probnotes.pdf) gives the measure-theoretic formulation, and [Peking University, §1.1.4](https://math.pku.edu.cn/teachers/lidf/course/stochproc/stochprocnotes/html/_book/prob.html) documents the Chinese name and probability axioms.

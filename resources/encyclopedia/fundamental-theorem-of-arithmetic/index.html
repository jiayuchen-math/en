Overview

Prime factorization supplies a coordinate system for positive integers under multiplication. Instead of viewing an integer as one large object, one records how many times each prime occurs. The fundamental theorem says both that these coordinates exist and that they do not depend on the sequence of divisions used to find them. Existence and uniqueness are separate assertions.

Prerequisites

A positive integer p>1p>1p>1 is prime if its only positive divisors are 111 and ppp. Know divisibility, induction, and the Bézout identity for coprime integers. The number 111 is not prime; this convention is necessary for uniqueness without arbitrary extra factors.

Definition and conventions

Every integer n>1n>1n>1 can be written

n=∏ppvp(n),n=\prod_{p}p^{v_p(n)},n=p∏​pvp​(n),

where each exponent is a nonnegative integer and only finitely many are nonzero. The exponents are uniquely determined by nnn. Equivalently, any two expressions as products of positive primes differ only in the ordering of factors. For n=1n=1n=1, the empty product is used. A negative integer additionally has a sign, while zero has no finite prime factorization of this kind.

Intuition

Primes are indivisible building blocks within positive integer multiplication. The uniqueness statement is stronger than the ability to split a composite number repeatedly. In another ring, irreducible elements may exist and factorizations may terminate, yet different factorizations need not be related by rearrangement or units.

Important properties

Existence follows by induction: an integer is prime or is a product of two smaller positive integers greater than one, to which induction applies. For uniqueness, the key lemma says that if a prime ppp divides ababab, it divides aaa or bbb. If it does not divide aaa, then gcd⁡(p,a)=1\gcd(p,a)=1gcd(p,a)=1; multiplying a Bézout identity by bbb proves p∣bp\mid bp∣b.

Apply this lemma to match one prime from the first factorization with a prime in the second, cancel it, and repeat. Thus a∣ba\mid ba∣b for positive integers exactly when vp(a)≤vp(b)v_p(a)\le v_p(b)vp​(a)≤vp​(b) for every prime. The gcd takes the smaller exponents and the lcm takes the larger ones.

Connections and further reading

Unique factorization domains abstract this arithmetic pattern. Rings of integers of number fields may fail element factorization, leading instead to prime ideal factorization and class groups. Li, Chapter 5 places the integer theorem in its algebraic setting.
